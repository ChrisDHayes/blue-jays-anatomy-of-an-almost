Act I · The story

I’d seen a game
turn before.

When the last out came, I felt how much I’d let myself believe. I’d watched the Jays find their way back into enough games that by November I expected them to do it again. I’d already been thinking about the story my son and I might get to tell about that night.

For me, the Blue Jays began with Jesse Barfield, Lloyd Moseby and Dave Stieb. I grew up watching those teams, then got to see Toronto win it all in 1992 and again in 1993. I wanted my son to know what it felt like to live through a Jays run of his own.

I was at Rogers Centre for the bat flip in 2015. I remember watching a throw back to the pitcher take a strange bounce and give Texas the lead. Then, in the bottom half of the same inning, José Bautista hit it out. I got to see the swing, the flip and the crowd erupt.

Ten years later, my son and I got our own October. I knew what a run like that could feel like as a kid. This time I got to watch it with him. By Game 7, I could already imagine the memory we might take from that night.

After the last out, I still wanted to know what we’d just seen. Was the whole run a fluke? How did the Jays get close enough for me to feel so sure?

Act II · The forecast

What did February miss?

I work in data and AI. When a result surprises me, I want to know what we expected at the time, what changed and how much of the pattern might come down to chance. I wanted to bring my data science background to the question I’d been asking as a fan.

So I went back to February, before anyone knew how the season would end. The had Toronto winning 82 games, about half its schedule. The Jays won 94. [1] How did twelve extra wins look from the batter’s box?

I turned to a separate set of preseason hitter forecasts. I wanted to see who gave the Jays more than February had suggested.

The Jays had outscored their opponents over a whole season and won more of the one-run games than they lost. I was starting to understand how they’d reached 94 wins. I still wondered why so many of those wins had come all at once.

How rare was ten in a row?

Ten straight wins felt extraordinary. To put that feeling to a test, I kept the same 94 wins and 68 losses and 150,000 times. I wanted to see how often a streak that long could show up in a season with that record.

What the season tells us ↓

Epilogue

How they got that close.

I came away with an answer. The Jays got more power from several hitters than February’s forecasts suggested. Springer stood out, and Barger, Varsho and others beat their projections too. [2] Over the season, the Jays scored 798 runs and allowed 721. They won 27 of the 47 games decided by a run. [3] That’s how a team forecast to win 82 games built a 94-win season and gave itself a shot at a title.

The streak was a question about timing. Once I held those 94 wins fixed, a streak at least as long turned up in more than one in five versions of the season. [4] What felt extraordinary in July was easier to understand once I saw the whole season.

In October I could see the contribution from the mound and the field too. Toronto’s pitchers struck out 25.2% of the batters they faced, compared with 17.1% for their opponents. When the ball was fielded, the Jays recorded an out on 316 of 422 plays. That was 74.9%, compared with 68.8% for the teams they faced. [8]

Going back through the season changed how I saw that last inning. The Jays had spent months giving themselves chances. When Guerrero got to second in the 11th, they had another one, and its outcome still depended on the next few plays. I think back to 2015, when a strange bounce and a great swing changed the same inning. Skill and chance were both there. That’s part of what I love about baseball.

The celebration we’d pictured never came. I can still remember getting ready to go downtown with my son. I’d wanted to share a Blue Jays season with him that felt like the ones I grew up with. We got to share this one all the way to the last out.

Sources and methods

The scores and play states are recorded outcomes. The preseason forecasts supply benchmarks. The shuffle tests the order of a fixed record, and the optional Game 7 probability is a model estimate.

  1. The 82-win team forecast

    FanGraphs’ February 2025 start-of-spring ZiPS standings projected Toronto at 82–80. The line in the season chart is 82 × games played ÷ 162. It represents a constant pace to the preseason total.

  2. The hitter comparison

    The February 24, 2025 Baseball Guru workbook supplies the hitter forecasts. The regular-season Toronto batting lines were reconstructed from Retrosheet in DS-04 v1.6. The default plot shows all 21 matched hitters. Nine of the 12 with at least 200 actual plate appearances beat their Guru slugging forecasts. The Whiffs archive supplies seven matches each for ZiPS and Steamer. Their exact preseason dates are unverified.

    Slugging is total bases divided by at-bats. The gap is actual slugging minus the selected forecast and rank uses the displayed sample. The profile rounds actual slugging × at-bats to recover actual total bases. The Guru difference in total bases uses the same actual at-bats for both rates.

    The 162 regular-season finals and 18 postseason finals were checked against Retrosheet by date, opponent, venue and score. The season chart uses the 162 regular-season games in order.   4. The streak and the shuffle

    The longest observed streak was 10 wins, and the best 30-game stretch had 23 wins. Phase 8 ran 150,000 random reorderings of the same 94 wins and 68 losses using NumPy and seed 20261114. For each ordering it took the maximum across the whole season. A streak of at least 10 appeared in 22.524% of shuffles. A 30-game stretch with at least 23 wins appeared in 35.6593%.

    Each shuffle strips away opponent, venue, starter and roster context. The percentages describe how often the two patterns occur given that final record.

  7. Run expectancy, win probability and the bunt

    Run expectancy. Phase 9 estimated runs remaining for 24 runner-and-out states using completed half-innings from all 2,430 MLB regular-season games in the 2025 Retrosheet archive. A play’s RE24 equals runs scored plus expected runs after the play minus expected runs before it. After-expectancy is zero when the half-inning or game ends. Batting RE24 sums the plate-appearance values. Dividing by actual plate appearances and multiplying by 100 gives the displayed rate.

    Experimental win probability. Phase 9 fitted a histogram gradient-boosted tree model to 189,939 pre-play states from those regular-season games. The inputs describe the inning, score, outs and runners. Regular-season checks held out entire games in five folds. The Game 7 trace applies that regular-season model to a postseason game, where extra-inning runner rules differ. Postseason calibration is pending.

    The sacrifice. The −0.222 figure is the change in expected runs for the recorded bunt, from a runner on second and no outs to a runner on third and one out. Comparing the bunt with swinging away would require outcome distributions for both choices from that start state, followed through the rest of the inning or game. The alternative remains a proposed analysis.

Metric definitions

ZiPS forecast
A preseason projection. February’s ZiPS team forecast put Toronto at 82 wins.
Slugging (SLG)
Total bases from hits divided by at-bats. A home run counts for four bases and a single counts for one.
Slugging gap
Actual slugging minus the forecast. A positive gap means the hitter beat his projection.
Plate appearances (PA)
Completed turns at the plate. Walks and hit-by-pitches count as plate appearances.
At-bats and total bases
At-bats count batting outcomes such as hits and outs. Total bases credit singles through home runs with one through four bases.
Extra total bases at actual at-bats
How many more or fewer total bases the hitter recorded than Guru projected at the same actual number of at-bats.
Run expectancy and RE24
Run expectancy is the average runs left in the half-inning. RE24 counts runs scored and the change in that average.
RE24 per 100 PA
Batting RE24 scaled to 100 plate appearances so hitters with different playing time can be compared.
Run differential
Total runs scored minus runs allowed. Toronto’s 2025 regular-season difference was 798 minus 721, or +77.
One-run games
Games decided by exactly one run. Toronto won 27 and lost 20 in the regular season.
Forecast pace
A straight line to 82 wins, spreading the February forecast evenly over 162 games.
Shuffle
A random reordering of the same wins and losses. It shows how often long winning stretches appear with that record fixed.
Experimental win probability
The model’s estimate of Toronto’s chance to win from a game state. It was trained on the regular season.
